\chapter{Phase 5A --- ML Acceleration Track}\label{ch:mlaccel}

\weeks{17--22} \quad Milestone M4 (Track A)

Track A brings the \emph{control plane} into hardware. Your thesis found that
the six intent classes are linearly separable in the sentence-embedding space,
which means the classifier and severity heads are tiny matrix--vector products.
The expensive part is the encoder that produces the embedding. This track
builds a small, verified int8 accelerator for the heads, then attacks the
encoder with one of two research routes.

\begin{whybox}
This track answers RQ4 and completes H3. It also teaches the core of ML
hardware: quantisation, multiply--accumulate arrays, dataflows, memory
bandwidth, and the roofline view of performance. These are the same concepts
used in commercial neural-network accelerators~\cite{sze2017efficient,jouppi2017tpu},
practised at a scale you can finish in six weeks.
\end{whybox}

\section{Characterise the control-plane workload}\label{sec:ml:workload}

Start from \cref{tab:workload:control}. Encoder compute is about 2.4~GFLOP per
sentence for mpnet and about 0.3~GFLOP for MiniLM (estimates). The heads need a
few thousand multiply--accumulates. Two consequences shape this track:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Throughput is irrelevant; latency and energy per inference
  matter.} A user complains at most every few seconds. A few hundred milliseconds
  per sentence feels instant. What matters is how much energy each inference
  costs, and whether the model fits in on-chip memory.
  \item \textbf{Memory bandwidth dominates large models.} The Zynq-7020 has
  roughly 630\,KB of block RAM. Even int8 MiniLM (about 23\,MB) cannot live on
  chip, so its weights must stream from DDR for every sentence. The roofline
  model~\cite{williams2009roofline} makes this precise. Performance is bounded
  by $\min(\text{peak compute},\ \text{bandwidth}\times\text{arithmetic
  intensity})$. A matrix--vector product reuses each weight once, about one MAC
  per weight byte at int8, so it is bandwidth-bound. Processing all of a
  sentence's roughly 14 tokens together reuses each weight 14 times.
\end{itemize}

\begin{conceptbox}{Int8 affine quantisation}
A real value $r$ is represented by an integer $q$ via $r \approx s\,(q-z)$, with
a scale $s$ and a zero point $z$~\cite{jacob2018quantization}. For weights,
symmetric quantisation ($z=0$) with one scale per output row is standard. A dot
product becomes an int8$\times$int8 product accumulated in int32, then rescaled
once: $\sum_i r_{w,i}r_{x,i}\approx s_w s_x\sum_i q_{w,i}q_{x,i}$. The rescale
can itself be a fixed-point multiply and shift, so inference is integer-only.
\end{conceptbox}

\section{Route A (required): the \texttt{head\_engine}}\label{sec:ml:head}

\begin{stepbox}[label=st:ml:headchoice]{Choose the head and freeze its integer model}
From your Phase~1 results (\cref{ch:codesign}), choose between logistic
regression (6 rows) and the one-vs-one SVM (15 rows plus voting). Unless the SVM
is clearly more accurate after quantisation, choose logistic regression: it has
2.5$\times$ fewer MACs and a simple margin confidence (O9). Fold the Ridge
severity weights in as a seventh row, giving one $7\times768$ int8 matrix of
\qty{5.25}{\kibi\byte}.

Write \file{nlp_hw/head_model.py}: a bit-exact integer model that takes the
same int8 inputs as the hardware and returns the class, a margin and a severity
in Q0.15 (clipped to $[0,1]$). This is the head's golden model.

\textbf{Check:} on the thesis test split, the integer model's macro-F1 and MAE
equal your Phase~1 int8 results. \textbf{Effort:} 1 day.
\end{stepbox}

\begin{stepbox}[label=st:ml:headrtl]{Design and build the engine}
A simple, effective design:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Weight memory.} One block RAM, $P$ int8 values wide. Load it
        once from the ARM over AXI.
  \item \textbf{Input buffer.} 768 int8 values, written by the ARM per
        sentence.
  \item \textbf{Datapath.} A \code{mac\_array} of $P$ lanes ($P=8$ or 16). Each
        cycle it multiplies $P$ weights by $P$ inputs and adds them, with an
        adder tree, into one int32 accumulator for the current row.
  \item \textbf{Post-processing.} After each row: add the bias, rescale with a
        fixed-point multiplier, and track the running argmax and the
        second-best score for the margin.
\end{itemize}
Cycles per inference $\approx 7\times768/P$: 672 cycles for $P=8$, which is
\qty{6.7}{\micro\s} at \qty{100}{\MHz}. Expose \reg{start}, \reg{done},
\reg{class}, \reg{margin} and \reg{severity} registers.

\textbf{Check:} cocotb against \file{nlp_hw/head_model.py} on all 1106
sentences: identical class, margin and severity. Lint-clean; resource log
updated. \textbf{Effort:} 1--1.5 weeks.
\end{stepbox}

\begin{stepbox}[label=st:ml:headint]{Integrate on the PYNQ}
Add \code{head\_engine} to the FPGA design next to \code{ise\_top}. The ARM, or
your laptop in option~A, now sends only the 768-value embedding. The hardware
returns class and severity, and the parameter mapping writes the enhancer
registers.

\textbf{Check:} end-to-end, typed complaint to audible change, now with the
heads in hardware. Measured latency of the head is negligible next to the
encoder. Say so in your report, with numbers. \textbf{Effort:} 2--3 days.
\end{stepbox}

\section{Then choose one research route}\label{sec:ml:routes}

\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item[Route B --- compress the text encoder]
  Keep typed or recognised text, and make the encoder small enough for edge
  hardware. Lower risk: everything can be measured on a laptop, and the
  hardware deliverable is a matrix-multiply engine.
  \item[Route C --- recognise intent directly from audio]
  Replace text plus encoder with a small network that classifies spoken
  complaints from audio features computed by \emph{your own} STFT. This is closer
  to a real hearing aid and removes the speech-recognition step the thesis
  listed as a limitation. It is also more data-hungry and riskier.
\end{description}

\subsection{Route B steps}

\begin{stepbox}[label=st:ml:minilm]{Swap and quantise the encoder}
Re-embed the thesis dataset with \code{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}~\cite{wang2020minilm},
retrain the heads on the thesis split, and measure macro-F1. Apply dynamic int8
quantisation to the encoder's linear layers, with PyTorch or ONNX Runtime, and
measure again. Integer-only transformer inference is
possible~\cite{kim2021ibert,zafrir2019q8bert}, but start with the easy wins.

\textbf{Check:} a table of encoder, precision, macro-F1, MAE, parameter count,
model size and CPU latency per sentence. This is the start of your RQ4
trade-off curve. \textbf{Effort:} 2--3 days.
\end{stepbox}

\begin{stepbox}[label=st:ml:distil]{Distil a tiny domain-specific student}
Your domain is narrow: complaints about hearing-aid sound. Train a small
student, for example 2--4 transformer layers of width 128, or even a small CNN
over word embeddings, to \emph{reproduce the mpnet embeddings} (an MSE or
cosine loss on the embedding). Use a large set of unlabelled, in-domain
sentences, generated in the same way as the thesis dataset. Then put the
unchanged thesis heads on top.

A domain vocabulary of a few thousand words keeps the embedding table small
enough for on-chip memory. That is a co-design decision worth reporting.

\textbf{Check:} a curve of macro-F1 against parameter count, from mpnet through
MiniLM down to your students. Note where it falls below the RQ4 target.
\textbf{Effort:} 1--1.5 weeks.
\end{stepbox}

\begin{stepbox}[label=st:ml:matmul]{A matrix-multiply engine for the student}
Map the student's matrix multiplies onto a small \emph{weight-stationary
systolic array}~\cite{kung1982systolic,chen2017eyeriss}. For example, a
$4\times4$ or $8\times8$ grid of int8 MAC cells: weights stay in the cells, and
activations flow through. Implement \emph{one tile} in RTL, verify it with cocotb
against numpy, and estimate cycles for the whole student from the tile's
throughput and the layer shapes.

Tool-based alternatives: hls4ml~\cite{duarte2018hls4ml,ch12-13:hls4mldocs} and
FINN with Brevitas~\cite{umuroglu2017finn,brevitas}. Check their current support
for the layer types you use, especially attention, before relying on them.

\textbf{Check:} the tile is bit-exact. The cycle and memory estimate for the
full student is backed by the tile measurement. \textbf{Effort:} 1.5 weeks.
\end{stepbox}

\subsection{Route C steps}

\begin{stepbox}[label=st:ml:audiodata]{Build a spoken-intent dataset}
No spoken hearing-aid-complaint dataset exists, so you will build one:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item synthesise the 1106 complaint sentences with several text-to-speech
        voices;
  \item mix them with noise at several SNRs, for example DEMAND noises as in
        VoiceBank+DEMAND;
  \item record a small \emph{real} test set from volunteers, with informed
        consent (\cref{ch:evaluation}).
\end{enumerate}
Consider pre-training on a public spoken-language-understanding dataset such as
Fluent Speech Commands~\cite{lugosch2019fsc}, or on keyword spotting with
Speech Commands~\cite{warden2018speechcommands}.

\textbf{Check:} train, development and test splits with no speaker or sentence
overlap, and a real-speech test set kept separate. \textbf{Effort:} 1 week.
\end{stepbox}

\begin{stepbox}[label=st:ml:dscnn]{A tiny audio-intent model on your own features}
Compute log-mel features from the STFT magnitudes your enhancer already
produces, with the mel filterbank as a sparse ROM. Note the tension: the
enhancer's short low-latency frames give coarse frequency resolution. Test
whether that is enough, or whether a separate longer analysis window is needed.

Train a depthwise-separable CNN of the kind used for keyword
spotting~\cite{zhang2017helloedge}, with tens of thousands of parameters rather
than millions. Use quantisation-aware training (for example Brevitas), and
deploy it with hls4ml or FINN, or with your own MAC array.

\textbf{Check:} accuracy against SNR on synthetic speech, and separately on real
recordings. Report the gap honestly; generalising from TTS to real voices is the
main risk. \textbf{Effort:} 2 weeks.
\end{stepbox}

\begin{pitfallbox}[Pitfalls in Track A]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Optimising the heads too much.} They are not the bottleneck. The
        \code{head\_engine} is a learning vehicle and a verified building block;
        the research question is in the encoder or the audio model.
  \item \textbf{Leaking the test split.} Distillation and TTS data must never
        contain test sentences.
  \item \textbf{Mismatched quantisation between training and hardware.} If your
        quantised model and your RTL round differently, F1 in Python means
        nothing. Keep a bit-exact integer model, as in Phase~1.
  \item \textbf{Counting FLOPs and ignoring memory.} For batch-1 inference,
        moving weights usually costs more time and energy than arithmetic.
\end{itemize}
\end{pitfallbox}
